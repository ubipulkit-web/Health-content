# 1. High blood pressure: what your numbers mean and what you can do about it

Consumer health condition article | Plain-English medical communication

High blood pressure is common, and most people who have it do not feel unwell. But over time, it can put extra strain on your heart, blood vessels and other organs.

The good news is that high blood pressure can usually be managed. Lifestyle changes, regular monitoring and medicines when needed can all help reduce your risk of future health problems.

## What is blood pressure?

Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against the walls of your arteries as your heart pumps it around your body.

A blood pressure reading contains two numbers, for example 130/80 mmHg.

The first number is your systolic blood pressure. This is the pressure when your heart contracts and pumps blood.

The second number is your diastolic blood pressure. This is the pressure when your heart relaxes between beats.

## What counts as high blood pressure?

Blood pressure can vary during the day, so one raised reading does not always mean that you have high blood pressure.

In the UK, blood pressure is usually considered high if it is:

- 140/90 mmHg or higher when measured by a healthcare professional

- 135/85 mmHg or higher when measured at home

If your reading is high, you may be asked to take several readings at home or wear a monitor for 24 hours. This helps your healthcare professional work out whether your blood pressure is consistently raised.

## Does high blood pressure cause symptoms?

Usually, it does not.

This is why high blood pressure is sometimes described as a "silent" condition. You can feel completely well while your blood pressure remains higher than it should be.

Rarely, people may experience symptoms such as:

- headaches

- blurred vision

- chest discomfort

These symptoms can have many causes, so they cannot be used to diagnose high blood pressure.

The only reliable way to know your blood pressure is to have it measured.

## Why does high blood pressure matter?

If your blood pressure stays high for a long time, it can damage blood vessels and increase the workload on your heart.

This can increase your risk of conditions including:

- heart disease

- heart attack

- stroke

- heart failure

- kidney disease

- some types of vascular dementia

Your overall risk depends on more than your blood pressure alone. Healthcare professionals may also consider factors such as your age, cholesterol levels, smoking status, family history and whether you have other conditions such as diabetes.

Even a modest reduction in blood pressure can be beneficial.

## Who is more likely to develop high blood pressure?

Your chances of developing high blood pressure generally increase as you get older.

Other factors that can increase your risk include:

- having a family history of high blood pressure

- eating a diet high in salt

- being overweight or living with obesity

- smoking

- drinking too much alcohol

- being physically inactive

- experiencing long-term stress

People from some ethnic backgrounds, including Black African, Black Caribbean and South Asian backgrounds, may also have a higher risk.

Some medical conditions and medicines can contribute to high blood pressure too.

## How can I lower my blood pressure?

### Eat less salt

Eating too much salt can increase blood pressure. Try to reduce foods that are high in salt and avoid automatically adding salt at the table. Remember that much of the salt we eat is already contained in packaged and processed foods rather than being added during cooking. Checking food labels can help you compare products.

### Be physically active

Regular activity can help lower blood pressure and support your heart and overall health. Adults should generally aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity each week, or around 75 minutes of vigorous activity. You do not need to complete this all at once. Walking, cycling, swimming and many other activities can count. If you have a medical condition or have not exercised for a long time, speak to a healthcare professional if you are unsure what level of activity is suitable for you.

### Maintain a healthy weight

If you are overweight, losing even some weight may help improve your blood pressure and reduce your risk of other health conditions. Try to focus on changes you can maintain rather than short-term diets.

### Limit alcohol

Regularly drinking more than the recommended limits can raise your blood pressure. Men and women are advised not to regularly drink more than 14 units of alcohol a week. If you drink 14 units, spreading them across several days is preferable to drinking them all at once.

### Stop smoking

Smoking does not directly cause every case of high blood pressure, but it significantly increases your risk of heart attack, stroke and other cardiovascular disease. Stopping smoking is one of the most effective things you can do for your long-term health.

## Will I need medicine?

Not everyone with high blood pressure needs medication straight away.

Your healthcare professional may recommend medicine if:

- your blood pressure is very high

- lifestyle changes have not lowered it enough

- you have a higher risk of heart attack or stroke

- you have another health condition that increases your cardiovascular risk

There are several types of blood pressure medicine. Which medicine is suitable for you depends on factors such as your age, other medical conditions and how high your blood pressure is. Some people need more than one medicine.

Do not stop prescribed blood pressure medication without speaking to your doctor, even if your readings improve.

## Can I check my blood pressure at home?

Yes. Home monitoring can be useful because it gives several readings over time rather than relying on a single measurement.

For a more accurate reading:

- sit quietly for several minutes before measuring

- sit with your back supported

- keep your feet flat on the floor

- support your arm

- avoid talking while the reading is being taken

- follow the instructions supplied with your monitor

Try to take readings under similar conditions each time. Your healthcare professional can tell you how often they want you to check your blood pressure.

## When should I seek urgent medical help?

High blood pressure itself usually does not cause an emergency. However, symptoms such as severe or persistent chest pain, severe shortness of breath, collapse or symptoms of a stroke need urgent assessment.

**Call 999 if you have severe chest pain that does not go away, particularly if it spreads to your arm, neck, jaw, back or stomach, or is accompanied by sweating, nausea, breathlessness or feeling light-headed.**

You should also call 999 if someone develops sudden facial weakness, weakness in an arm or difficulty speaking.

## Key points

- High blood pressure usually causes no symptoms, so checking it is important.

- A reading of 140/90 mmHg or above in a healthcare setting, or 135/85 mmHg or above at home, is generally considered high.

- Lifestyle changes can make a real difference, although some people also need medication.

- Managing your blood pressure reduces your risk of serious conditions such as heart attack and stroke.
